1. Introduction

Forests play a critical role in the maintenance of ecological equilibrium, the preservation of biodiversity, and the advancement of global sustainability. They provide indispensable ecosystem services, including carbon sequestration, soil stabilization, and hydrological regulation, while serving as a habitat for over 80% of the world's terrestrial species. Beyond their ecological functions, forests underpin the livelihoods and cultural identities of millions, providing essential resources ranging from timber to non-timber forest products. Furthermore, forests are fundamental to the realization of international mandates, most notably the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) pertaining to climate action, biodiversity conservation, and the development of sustainable communities (Hoang and Kanemoto, 2020; Sotirov et al., 2020). Tropical trees, in particular, are estimated to contribute 23% of the total climate mitigation required to fulfill the Paris Agreement objectives by 2030 (Gibbs et al., 2018).

Despite their critical importance, forests are under intensifying pressure from deforestation and degradation driven by agricultural expansion, rapid urbanization, industrial logging, and the impacts of climate change. These environmental challenges are complicated by the fragmented architecture of international forest governance and a persistent lack of legally binding global agreements (Sotirov et al., 2020).

As noted by Fernández-Blanco et al. (2019), governance structures have evolved to include a diverse array of mechanisms that extend beyond traditional state-centered approaches. This evolution has led to the emergence of environmental regime complexes: intricate webs of multiple, interacting actors, networks, and institutions that regulate a specific issue area through overlapping regimes. Many scholars argue that rather than a unified global policy, forest governance is best characterized as a "regime complex" (Sotirov et al., 2020). This state is defined by significant connections between specific, relatively narrow regimes, but suffers from the "absence of an overall architecture or hierarchy that structures the whole set."

This assignment seeks to untangle the complexities of the international forest regime complex. By mapping the regime, it aims to visualize its current state and facilitate a discussion on its core components, the nature of its institutional interactions, and its overarching global significance.

2. Mapping the Forest Regime Complex

A. Methodological Considerations

The mapping process focused on the intersections between the forest sector and adjacent regimes to illustrate the fragmented distribution of policies across global issue areas.

The Forest Regime Complex comprises a network of actors that collectively govern forests on a global scale. While the map captures primary international organizations and multilateral initiatives, certain actors are implicitly rather than explicitly represented. For instance, national governments, the primary enforcers of forest law, and regional management bodies are essential but omitted for visual clarity. Similarly, while NGOs, civil society groups, Indigenous groups, private investors, Carbon Market actors, scientific and academic institutions are not individually listed, their influence is subsumed within broader categories. For example, the REDD+ Programme entry inherently represents a vast network of funding bodies, civil society groups, and technical experts.

B. Main Results

At the core of this regime complex lies the United Nations Forum on Forests (UNFF), an intergovernmental platform established in 2000 to champion Sustainable Forest Management (SFM). The UNFF prioritizes the conservation, restoration, and sustainable utility of forest ecosystems, anchored by its four primary objectives and six global goals (Fernández-Blanco et al., 2019). A fundamental output of this forum is the UN Forest Instrument, a voluntary framework providing the principles and guidelines necessary for SFM. Supporting the UNFF's mandate are the International Arrangement on Forests (IAF) and the Collaborative Partnership on Forests (CPF), which coordinate actions among major international organizations to foster policy coherence and facilitate state-NGO partnerships. Critical financial pillars of the IAF include the Global Forest Financing and Facilitation Network (GFFFN) and the Trust Fund. More recently, member states solidified this trajectory by adopting the UN Strategic Plan for Forests 2017–2030.

Tracing the historical development of the regime, the 1992 UNCED saw the adoption of two non-binding documents: the Rio Forest Principles and Agenda 21, which established action plans for sustainability. While seminal institutions such as the UNFCCC, CBD, and UNCCD were founded during this period, they were designed for adjacent regimes; consequently, no legally binding treaty was ever established exclusively for forests (Sotirov et al., 2020; Reischl, 2012).

Going back in time, three conventions from the 1970s began indirectly influencing forest governance: the Ramsar Convention on Wetlands, the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES), and the World Heritage Convention. In the 1980s, the International Tropical Timber Organisation (ITTO) was formed, leading to the International Tropical Timber Agreement (ITTA) in 1983. The ITTO operates at the intersection of economy and ecology, seeking to balance timber trade benefits with conservation through sustainable practices (Fernández-Blanco et al., 2019).

The climate regime, steered by the UNFCCC, is a pivotal force in forest governance, effectively tethering forest health to global climate policy. A cornerstone of this intersection is REDD+ (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation), which provides financial incentives for countries to preserve and restore forests. To prevent negative externalities, REDD+ includes safeguards to protect biodiversity and indigenous rights, thereby minimizing conflict with the CBD. Implementation is further bolstered by the Green Climate Fund, while the World Bank's Forest Carbon Partnership Facility (FCPF) and Forest Investment Programme (FIP) serve as vital multilateral funding streams. Notably, a synergy exists between REDD+ and the UNFF: while the former provides the financial impetus for protection, the latter offers the comprehensive framework for sustainable management.

Under the biodiversity regime, the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) identifies forests as primary reservoirs of global biodiversity. Initiatives like the Program of Work (POW) promote sustainable management, while the Nagoya Protocol governs access to genetic resources. Nationally, NBSAPs ensure these global forest conservation goals are integrated into domestic legislation. While REDD+ and the CBD often share goals of carbon sequestration and habitat preservation, tensions can emerge if carbon storage is prioritized at the expense of ecological diversity or indigenous land rights (Fernández-Blanco et al., 2019).

The Land and Soil Regime, represented by the UNCCD and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), also converges with forest governance. Forest restoration is viewed as a primary strategy to combat land degradation, aligning directly with UNFF objectives. Together, the FAO and UNCCD provide the evidence-based policymaking and capacity-building essential to the broader regime (Braatz, n.d.).

Market-driven mechanisms like the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) and the Programme for the Endorsement of Forest Certification (PEFC) introduce voluntary, standards-based governance. The FSC is distinguished by its rigorous sustainability benchmarks, whereas the PEFC offers a flexible, industry-aligned alternative. Despite their competition for market share, both systems remain consistent with the SFM goals defined by the UNFF and ITTO. Additionally, the Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention (ILO C169) serves as a critical legal pillar, recognizing indigenous peoples as the vital stewards and necessary participants in forest governance (Fernández-Blanco et al., 2019).

Interactions within the International Forest Regime Complex reveal a spectrum of synergies and conflicts. A "façade of synergy" — maintained by general principles — often masks deep-seated tensions when concrete objectives are pursued. (Fernández-Blanco et al., 2019)

Synergies typically appear among high-level, broad institutional elements — such as Agenda 21 — which promote general sustainability without demanding specific concessions. In contrast, conflicts emerge when goals become substantive and precise. For example, the carbon-centric goals of REDD+ may clash with the biodiversity mandates of the CBD or the rights-based frameworks of ILO C169. Similarly, the ITTO's focus on timber production can create friction with the conservationist mandates of CITES.

3. Conclusion

The mapping of this complex reveals that a significant number of actors within Global Forest Governance are actually rooted in external regimes, specifically those concerning climate, biodiversity, and land degradation (such as the UNFCCC and CBD). This interconnected structure underscores the extent to which forest governance is inextricably linked to critical global challenges, including climate change, biodiversity loss, desertification, and the protection of indigenous rights.

The Forest Regime Complex serves as a reflection of the fragmented yet deeply intertwined nature of global environmental policy. While these institutional interactions frequently yield synergies, they also generate inevitable conflicts arising from divergent priorities and competing interests. Achieving enhanced coherence and coordination among these diverse actors remains a formidable challenge; however, it also represents a vital opportunity to improve forest governance. Effectively navigating this complex is essential for reversing deforestation, conserving biodiversity, mitigating climate change, and securing the rights of local and indigenous communities.
